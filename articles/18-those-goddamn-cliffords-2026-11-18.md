# Those Goddamn Cliffords
### How a bunch of nineteenth-century mathematicians got mixed up in an investigation of the entire universe—and refused to leave

*Mathematics and Elite Squad Dynamics · Dispatch 18 · Scientific satire · 18 November 2026*

*By Matthew Chenoweth Wright | Monolithic LLC*

There comes a moment in every scientific investigation when you realize you've made a terrible mistake.

Not a mathematical mistake. Those are relatively easy to correct. You cross something out, apologize to the tensor, and move along.

No, I'm talking about the moment when you ask an innocent question—something like *“Why does everything seem to rotate?”*—and suddenly find yourself surrounded by nineteenth-century mathematicians demanding that you learn an entirely new algebra.

And at the front of the mob stands William Kingdon Clifford.

With his goddamn algebra.

Again.

## It started innocently enough

Look, I wasn't asking for trouble.

The Earth spins. Galaxies spin. Electrons possess spin. Hurricanes spin. My coffee spins when I stir it. My research program occasionally spins so violently that GitHub probably needs a seat belt.

Surely, I thought, there must be something interesting connecting all these things.

A perfectly reasonable question.

But mathematics has a nasty habit of responding to perfectly reasonable questions by opening a trapdoor beneath your feet.

Before long, ordinary geometry wasn't enough.

Vectors weren't enough.

Complex numbers weren't enough.

Somebody mentioned *geometric algebra*.

And there he was.

Clifford.

Smiling from 1878.

## Who invited this man?

William Kingdon Clifford was a British mathematician who apparently looked at the existing mathematical notation and decided it wasn't sufficiently dangerous.

He developed an algebraic framework in which geometric objects can be multiplied in ways that preserve information about their lengths, orientations, and relationships.

The central trick is deceptively simple.

Take two vectors and multiply them using the *geometric product*.

Instead of getting just a number, as with the dot product, or just an oriented object, as with the cross product, you get something that contains both kinds of information.

It's rather like asking two arrows to introduce themselves and discovering they've formed a limited-liability corporation.

A geometric product can be separated into a symmetric part, related to the ordinary dot product, and an antisymmetric part, called the *outer product*.

That outer product describes an oriented plane segment, or *bivector*.

And bivectors, it turns out, are extremely useful for describing rotations.

At this point, I was still willing to cooperate.

Then the rotors arrived.

## Rotors: because ordinary rotation wasn't complicated enough

Most people understand rotation perfectly well.

You turn a doorknob.

You spin a wheel.

You rotate your chair so you don't have to look at the equations anymore.

Mathematicians, however, were not content to leave things there.

They wanted objects called *rotors*.

A rotor is an algebraic object that can represent rotation through the geometric product.

Instead of pushing a vector around using a rotation matrix, you can perform a kind of algebraic sandwich:

**Rotor. Vector. Reverse rotor.**

And out comes the rotated vector.

I understand that this is elegant.

I understand that it is powerful.

I also understand that somewhere in the history of mathematics, someone looked at a perfectly serviceable rotation matrix and said:

“Not bad. But what if we made it a sandwich?”

Those goddamn Cliffords.

## Then things got weird

In three-dimensional space, rotors are closely related to another mathematical structure: the spin group.

And here the plot becomes wonderfully strange.

Ordinary objects return to their original orientation after a full 360-degree turn.

But certain spinor representations have a peculiar property: rotate them through 360 degrees, and their mathematical sign changes.

Another complete turn—720 degrees in total—restores the original spinor.

This is a real and important feature of spinor mathematics, associated with the fact that Spin(3) is a double cover of SO(3).

It does **not** mean an electron must physically somersault twice before being allowed back into the laboratory.

But it does mean the mathematics of quantum spin has deeper rotational structure than ordinary arrows on graph paper.

At this point, Clifford had moved from being an annoying historical mathematician to being a genuinely interesting suspect.

Which is exactly how they get you.

## Enter Professor Chaos

Naturally, I became suspicious.

Rotation shows up in classical mechanics.

It shows up in fluids.

It shows up in complex phases.

It shows up in quantum spinors.

It shows up in the geometry of curved spacetime.

Could all these appearances be shadows of something more fundamental?

Could there be a shared mathematical language beneath seemingly different physical phenomena?

Possibly.

Could that language help organize a research program investigating universal rotation?

Certainly.

Had I discovered that the entire universe was one enormous Clifford rotor?

**Absolutely not.**

And there's the rub.

A mathematical tool can describe many things without those things being manifestations of one physical mechanism.

A ruler measures a submarine and a sandwich. That does not mean submarines are sandwiches.

Likewise, Clifford algebra can describe spatial rotations and spinor transformations without proving that galactic rotation, quantum spin, and cosmic expansion come from one universal spinning engine.

A common mathematical language is not the same thing as a common physical cause.

Unfortunately, the Cliffords neglected to include this warning on their business cards.

## The Zoo enters the chat

This is where Monolithic's research took a turn for the wonderfully adversarial.

Rather than proclaiming that rotation explained everything, we began checking whether our proposed cosmic-rotation equations actually survived contact with established general relativity.

We constructed a public Rotation Laboratory.

We examined known rotating spacetimes.

We tested the mathematical relationships between rotation, curvature, matter, and cosmic expansion.

And then we invited the equations to misbehave.

They accepted enthusiastically.

Gödel's famous rotating universe refused to expand.

An expanding, rotating spacetime demanded a more complicated matter source than our simple candidates could supply.

Ordinary scalar fields were rejected under specific assumptions.

Electromagnetic fields failed.

A massive-vector theory called Proca theory joined the party, saw the strong energy condition, and promptly failed too—for the selected metric and source assumptions.

By the time we reached ROT-011, the ledger contained an impressive collection of mathematically precise reasons why our particular attempts hadn't worked.

Not reasons why universal rotation was impossible.

Not reasons why every rotating universe was wrong.

Just reasons why **those particular proposed explanations couldn't do what we had asked them to do**.

A crucial distinction.

Professor Chaos was delighted.

The equations were less enthusiastic.

## And what did the Cliffords have to say?

Nothing.

Absolutely nothing.

Because Clifford algebra is a mathematical framework, not an oracle.

It can tell us how rotations compose.

It can describe bivectors, spinors, and geometric transformations with remarkable elegance.

It can help us recognize relationships between mathematical structures that otherwise look unrelated.

But it cannot, by itself, tell us whether the observed universe possesses a nonzero global vorticity.

For that, we need a physically defined spacetime, an independently derived matter theory, measurable predictions, and observations capable of distinguishing the proposal from conventional cosmology.

In other words, we need physics.

Not merely beautiful algebra.

I find this deeply inconvenient.

## Why I still love the bastards

Here's the thing.

Despite all the trouble, Clifford algebra really is beautiful.

It brings geometry and algebra together in a way that can make familiar calculations feel surprisingly unified.

It helps explain why spinors behave differently from ordinary vectors.

It turns rotation into something that can be manipulated through a coherent mathematical structure.

And it offers a powerful language for discussing transformations across several branches of physics.

But the most valuable lesson of our investigation is not that the Cliffords secretly explain the universe.

It's that mathematical elegance must never be mistaken for physical evidence.

A theory doesn't become true because its notation is gorgeous.

It doesn't become universal because its vocabulary is versatile.

And it doesn't become a discovery because I put a crown on the equation and shout *MUAAH HA HA HA!*

Although that last part remains an essential research tradition.

## A final word to William Kingdon Clifford

Sir,

I admire your work.

Your algebra is magnificent.

Your rotors are elegant.

Your bivectors are unexpectedly useful.

Your mathematical descendants have given us an extraordinarily powerful way to think about geometry, transformations, and spin.

But I started this investigation with a perfectly innocent question about why things rotate.

Now I have a laboratory, a bibliography, a collection of exact Einstein-tensor calculations, a growing stack of negative results, and a research program that keeps discovering more things it doesn't yet know.

And every time I look under another mathematical rock, there you are.

Grinning.

Holding another bivector.

So here's the deal.

You may stay.

You may help us describe the geometry.

You may even have your own chapter.

But until we derive a physical mechanism and confirm it against nature, you are **not** allowed to declare that the entire universe is spinning.

Not even if you can write it as a beautiful exponential.

And for God's sake, stop touching the cosmological constant.

**Those goddamn Cliffords.**
