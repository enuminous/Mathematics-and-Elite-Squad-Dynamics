# Confessions of a Benchtesting Addict

*Mathematics and Elite Squad Dynamics · Dispatch 17 · Satire · 16 November 2026*

*A fictional confession. No laboratory experiment is represented as having occurred.*

My name is—well, that’s in the sealed allocation file—and I am a benchtesting addict.

I have never actually reached the bench.

This is not denial. It is a release criterion.

It started innocently. A hypothesis. A capacitor. A small mass. The wholesome conviction that somewhere between an elegant equation and a sufficiently sensitive instrument, nature would agree to a meeting.

I wanted ten micronewtons. Ten seemed modest. I was trying to be considerate to the universe.

Before long, I knew the sampling rate, the washout period, the blinding procedure, the name of the file containing the name of the person who would eventually hold the key, and the exact conditions under which nobody was permitted to open it.

I did not know what made the mass move.

But that was only one missing item, and there were several excellent tables.

The gateway drug was a column headed **Assumptions**. You think you can control it. One column. Just to keep things tidy. Then you wake up surrounded by acceptance matrices, asking whether breakfast has an independently calibrated endpoint.

My capacitor forgot exponentially. I found this moving.

The proof mass did not.

I put them fifty millimeters apart. This was close enough for a diagram but apparently still too far for a metaphor.

An experimentalist read the protocol.

“What connects the memory state to the force?”

“Section three.”

“Physically.”

“There’s an independent technical reviewer.”

“What would the reviewer be reviewing?”

I turned to the acceptance matrix. This question had not been assigned a row.

Naturally, I improved the statistics.

This is a common response among people with my condition. When confronted with a missing mechanism, we tighten a confidence interval. It creates an immediate sensation of scientific progress without requiring anything to accelerate.

First I asked whether the bench looked quiet enough. Then someone pointed out that a noisy bench occasionally looks quiet. My decision rule had taken its good mood as a character reference.

I replaced the point estimate with an upper confidence bound. This was a real correction. For several minutes, I experienced the unfamiliar pleasure of having fixed the thing I had actually been criticized for.

Unfortunately, the force law remained absent.

It was now absent with better statistical protection.

I developed Stage Zero.

Stage Zero was where the theory would acquire everything needed to become an experiment: a physical carrier, a coupling, a derivation, conservation accounting, a distinguishing prediction, and someone else willing to run the code.

It was a magnificent gate. You could have defended a medieval city with it.

Nothing could get through, including the project.

At first, passage required a reviewer’s signature. Then it emerged that I had not specified what the signature meant. So I replaced it with six explicit checks, all of which were unresolved.

This improved the situation enormously. Previously, we did not know why we could not begin. Now we could sort the reasons.

The laboratory asked what, precisely, it could quote.

“A decisive test of a new physical interaction.”

“Do you have the interaction?”

“We have a document explaining why we must have it.”

There was a silence with excellent vibration isolation.

Eventually we separated the work. The laboratory could measure what its instrument could detect. The theorist would have to produce something for it to detect. Neither deliverable could be substituted for the other by increasing the version number.

I found this division of labor unnecessarily physical.

Still, the instrument study had a useful property: it could finish. A report saying “this apparatus cannot meet your target” would be a completed job. Nobody had to discover a new interaction to earn the invoice.

The theory paper had a less convenient property: I had to write the theory.

Not the conditions under which I would accept the theory. Not the list of people authorized to acknowledge its existence. Not a color-coded diagram of the journey from promising intuition to withheld approval.

The steps that made the prediction follow.

I opened a blank document and typed **Force law**.

There was no checklist underneath it. No planned sample size. No reassuring little box marked PASS.

For the first time all afternoon, the blank space was doing useful work.

I sat with it.

Then I added a checkbox.

Recovery is a process.
